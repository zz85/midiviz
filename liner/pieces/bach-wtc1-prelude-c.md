# Overview

The first piece of [[well-tempered-clavier|The Well-Tempered Clavier]], and one of the most played pieces Bach wrote. Every bar is one chord, broken into the same sixteenth-note figure and played twice.

There is no tune in the usual sense. The music is in the chords themselves: they leave C major for G, come back by way of D minor, take a chromatic detour, and then sit for eight bars on a low G before settling on a low C.

It takes two to three minutes, and it is often among the first Bach pieces piano students learn. A century after Bach, Charles Gounod wrote a melody over it and turned it into the [[ave-maria|Ave Maria]].

# Sections

## m1 Home: C major
Four bars state the key: tonic, a chord on D, the dominant seventh, tonic again. The whole piece in miniature.

## m5 Out to G major
The bass steps down C–B–A and the harmony moves to G major, the dominant key, arriving in bar 11.

## m12 The way back
Two [[diminished-seventh|diminished seventh]] chords lead through D minor, then a plain cadence brings C major back in bar 19.

## m20 Chromatic detour
A B♭, an F♯, an A♭: the four bars that wander furthest from the key.

## m24 Dominant pedal
The bass holds G for eight bars while the chords above it tighten and relax.

## m32 Tonic pedal and close
The bass falls to low C and stays there to the end.

# Moments

## m1 · history
Bach finished Book I of The Well-Tempered Clavier in 1722. This prelude opens it.

He was Kapellmeister at the court of Köthen. The collection has 24 preludes and fugues, one in every major and minor key, climbing by semitones: C major, C minor, C♯ major, C♯ minor, and so on up to B minor.

The title page says it is for "the use and profit of musical youth desirous of learning", and for the pastime of those already skilled.

source: Bach's autograph title page (1722), in David Ledbetter, *Bach's Well-tempered Clavier: The 48 Preludes and Fugues* (Yale, 2002)

## m3 · analysis
Each bar is one chord, broken into the same figure and played twice.

The left hand plays the two lowest notes and holds them. The right hand plays the top three, twice over. Written out, every bar is a five-voice chord.

Bars 1–4 are I – ii⁴₂ – V⁶₅ – I, all over a C or a B in the bass: a complete cadence before the piece has gone anywhere.

## m5 · analysis
Bar 5 begins the move to G major, the dominant key.

A minor over C (bar 5), then D7 over C (bar 6). D7 is the dominant of G, so the ear now expects G. The bass walks down C–B–A, reaches D in bar 10, and lands on G in bar 11.

## m7 · trivia
Bach did not write this for the piano.

"Clavier" meant any keyboard: harpsichord, clavichord or organ. In 1722 the piano was a rare new instrument. Bach tried Gottfried Silbermann's pianos in the 1730s and is said to have found the treble weak; he approved of Silbermann's later models in the 1740s.

source: Christoph Wolff, *Johann Sebastian Bach: The Learned Musician* (Norton, 2000)

## m9 · history
An earlier, shorter version is in a notebook Bach began in 1720 for his eldest son.

The *Clavier-Büchlein vor Wilhelm Friedemann Bach* was started when Friedemann was nine. It holds early versions of eleven of the Book I preludes, this one among them.

source: Ledbetter (2002)

## m12 · analysis
A C♯ and a B♭ in bar 12: a [[diminished-seventh|diminished seventh]] chord that pulls toward D minor.

C♯–E–G–B♭ over a G bass resolves to D minor in bar 13. Bar 14 does the same thing one step lower: B–D–F–A♭ over F, resolving to C major over E in bar 15.

## m14 · history
"Well-tempered" does not have to mean equal temperament.

Bach's title says the keyboard is tuned so that all 24 keys can be played. In the 1720s that could be one of several unequal tunings, in which each key keeps a slightly different colour. Which one Bach used is still argued about.

source: Ledbetter (2002), chapter 2

## m16 · analysis
Bars 16–19 are the plainest cadence there is: IV – ii⁷ – V⁷ – I.

F major seventh over E, D minor seventh, G7, and C major in bar 19. The bass now plays an octave lower than at the start.

## m18 · trivia
As a boy in Bonn, Beethoven played this collection.

In 1783 his teacher Christian Gottlob Neefe wrote that the twelve-year-old "plays chiefly The Well-Tempered Clavier of Sebastian Bach, which Herr Neefe put into his hands", calling it "the non plus ultra of our art".

aside: Bonn, 1783 | The twelve-year-old Beethoven plays from this book.

source: Neefe, in Cramer's *Magazin der Musik* (March 1783), quoted in Thayer's *Life of Beethoven*

## m20 · analysis
A B♭ turns C major into C7, and the music leans toward F.

Bar 21 is F major seventh. Then the bass rises a semitone to F♯ (bar 22) and A♭ (bar 23), with diminished seventh chords above both.

## m22-m23 · history
Many 19th-century editions add a bar after this one. Bach did not write it.

screen: Bar 22 | Editors would later add a bar here. Bach never wrote it.

Bach goes straight from the F♯ diminished seventh (bar 22) to a diminished seventh over A♭ (bar 23). Editors around 1800 found the jump too abrupt and put a bar of their own in between. It is usually credited to Christian Friedrich Gottlieb Schwencke and stayed in popular editions for a century, so some copies have 36 bars. This performance has Bach's 35.

source: Ledbetter (2002); Alfred Dürr, *Johann Sebastian Bach: Das Wohltemperierte Klavier* (Bärenreiter, 1998)

## m24 · listen
Listen to the low G: it stays for the next eight bars.

This is a dominant [[pedal-point|pedal point]]. Above the G, the chords alternate between G7 and chords that clash with it, and each clash makes the return to G7 sound more like a question waiting for C.

## m26 · history
In 1932 Heinrich Schenker drew the whole prelude as one slow line falling to C.

His *Fünf Urlinie-Tafeln* (*Five Graphic Music Analyses*) reduce the piece layer by layer until only a stepwise descent in the top voice over I–V–I in the bass remains. It is still a standard example in music theory classes.

source: Heinrich Schenker, *Fünf Urlinie-Tafeln* (Vienna, 1932; Dover, 1969)

## m28 · analysis
Bar 28 is the most tense chord in the piece: F♯–A–C–E♭ over the low G.

It is a diminished seventh, and none of its notes belong to the G chord it sits on. Bar 29 releases it into C major over G, and bars 30–31 settle back onto G7.

## m30 · trivia
In 1853 Charles Gounod published a melody improvised over this prelude.

It first appeared as a *Méditation* for violin or cello with piano. Words from the Ave Maria were set to it in 1859, and the [[ave-maria|Bach–Gounod Ave Maria]] became one of the most popular songs of the 19th century. Gounod used an edition with the Schwencke bar.

aside: Paris, 1853 | Gounod will sing an Ave Maria over these chords.

source: Wikipedia, "Ave Maria (Bach/Gounod)"

## m32 · analysis
The bass reaches low C, two octaves below where it began, and holds it to the end.

Over this tonic pedal comes C7 (bar 32), F major (bar 33) and G7 (bar 34), then C major.

## m33.3 · listen
Listen to the figure: it has just changed, for the first time.

After 32 bars of the same shape, the right hand now rises and turns back down, and bar 34 runs up to the top before the last chord.

## m35 · history
The prelude ends on a plain C major chord. Next comes the four-voice Fugue in C major.

In both books each prelude is paired with a fugue in the same key.
