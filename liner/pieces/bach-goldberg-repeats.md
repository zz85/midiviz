# Overview

An Aria, thirty variations and the Aria once more: Bach turns one 32-bar harmonic plan into a 72-minute universe. The famous melody is not the theme in the ordinary sense. What survives is the bass and the sequence of harmonies beneath it, leaving every variation free to invent a new surface.

The architecture is exact. The thirty variations form ten groups of three. Every third variation is a canon, with the imitating voices moving progressively from the unison to the ninth; the other two places alternate character pieces, dances and brilliant writing conceived for one or two harpsichord manuals. Three variations—15, 21 and 25—move into G minor, and the French Overture at Variation 16 opens the second half.

Bach published the work in 1741 as the fourth and culminating volume of his *Clavier-Übung*, calling it simply an “Aria with diverse variations for harpsichord with two manuals.” The nickname came from Bach's first biographer, Johann Nikolaus Forkel, in 1802. Forkel said the young Johann Gottlieb Goldberg played the variations through the sleepless nights of Count Keyserlingk, but no dedication appears in the print and Goldberg was only fourteen in 1741; the Bach-Archiv treats the commission story as unlikely.

This MIDI plays every repeat: each half twice, with its first ending the first time and its second the second time, and the Aria da capo without its repeats, as it is usually played. It realizes the written ornaments and places 2.5 seconds between movements. The tempos are the editors' metronome marks of the Mutopia edition where it gives one, and for the 18 movements it leaves unmarked a tempo chosen for their character, all of them then played 10 % faster. Its changing velocities are rule-based interpretation of the score, not a captured pianist's performance.
