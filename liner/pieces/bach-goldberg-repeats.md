# Overview

An Aria, thirty variations and the Aria once more: Bach turns one 32-bar harmonic plan into an 85-minute universe. The famous melody is not the theme in the ordinary sense. What survives is the bass and the sequence of harmonies beneath it, leaving every variation free to invent a new surface.

The architecture is exact. The thirty variations form ten groups of three. Every third variation is a canon, with the imitating voices moving progressively from the unison to the ninth; the other two places alternate character pieces, dances and brilliant writing conceived for one or two harpsichord manuals. Three variations—15, 21 and 25—move into G minor, and the French Overture at Variation 16 opens the second half.

Bach published the work in 1741 as the fourth and culminating volume of his *Clavier-Übung*, calling it simply an “Aria with diverse variations for harpsichord with two manuals.” The nickname came from Bach's first biographer, Johann Nikolaus Forkel, in 1802. Forkel said the young Johann Gottlieb Goldberg played the variations through the sleepless nights of Count Keyserlingk, but no dedication appears in the print and Goldberg was only fourteen in 1741; the Bach-Archiv treats the commission story as unlikely.

This MIDI plays every repeat: each half twice, with its first ending the first time and its second the second time, and the Aria da capo without its repeats, as it is usually played. It realizes the written ornaments and places 2.5 seconds between movements. Its changing velocities are rule-based interpretation of the score, not a captured pianist's performance.

source: Bach-Archiv Leipzig, “Introduction” to the Goldberg Variations; Bach, first edition title page (Nuremberg, 1741); J. N. Forkel, *Über Johann Sebastian Bachs Leben, Kunst und Kunstwerke* (1802); Mutopia Project BWV 988 LilyPond editions

# Moments

## @0:00 · listen
Listen past the decorated melody: the slow bass and its harmonic rhythm are the real theme.

## @8:39.5 · analysis
Variation 3 begins the chain of canons: unison, second, third, onward to the ninth.

The canons occupy every third place. In each, one upper voice follows another while a separate bass continues the Aria's harmonic pattern.

source: Bach-Archiv Leipzig, “Introduction”

## @13:32 · technique
Variation 5 is the first piece explicitly exploiting two manuals and crossing hands.

On one harpsichord keyboard the hands collide; two manuals let each line keep its own level. On the piano, performers recreate the choreography on one keyboard.

source: Bach's 1741 first-edition headings; Bach-Archiv Leipzig

## @24:10.4 · analysis
Variation 10 is a fughetta: four voices enter with the same compact subject.

It stands at the opening of the fourth group of three, where Bach repeatedly changes style before the next virtuoso variation and canon.

## @67:07.8 · listen
Variation 25 stretches dissonances across the harmony until almost every step aches.

This *Adagio* is the last and longest of the three minor-key variations. Wanda Landowska later nicknamed it the “black pearl,” a name not found in Bach's score.

source: Bach's 1741 first edition; Landowska nickname reported in later Goldberg literature

## @70:22.3 · technique
Variation 26 superimposes 18/16 figuration on a 3/4 sarabande, then swaps the layers.

The pulse is shared, but the two hands appear to inhabit different meters.

## @72:40.4 · analysis
The canon at the ninth is the only canon without a separate bass voice.

Two fast lines still imply the familiar harmony while pursuing one another an octave and a second apart.

## @81:29 · history
At the expected canon at the tenth, Bach gives a quodlibet: learned counterpoint made from popular songs.

The tunes are “I have so long been away from you” and “Cabbage and turnips have driven me away.” Their homecoming jokes prepare the return of the Aria.

source: Bach-Archiv Leipzig, “Introduction”

screen: The final variation | No canon: two popular songs come home together.
