# Overview

The fifth pair of [[well-tempered-clavier|The Well-Tempered Clavier]], Book I. The prelude is a single figure kept going: the right hand runs in sixteenth notes over a bass that dances in detached eighths, until the last three bars break into a free, improvised close.

The fugue, in four voices, opens with a flourish: a rush of fast notes, then proud dotted rhythms. That gesture runs through almost the whole piece and ends it in full chords.

An earlier version of the prelude is in the keyboard book Bach began in 1720 for his eldest son, Wilhelm Friedemann.

source: Peter Benary, *J. S. Bachs Wohltemperiertes Klavier: Text – Analyse – Wiedergabe* (Aarau, 2005), pp. 28–29; the *Klavierbüchlein für Wilhelm Friedemann Bach*, No. 17, "Praeludium 4"

## @1:15 Fugue: four entries
The subject in each voice in turn, from the bass up.

## @1:41 Entries and episodes
The subject returns in new keys, between episodes built from its fast notes.

## @3:13 The close in chords
The last bars (25–27) in full four-voice chords and dotted rhythms.

# Moments

## @0:01 · listen
The right hand never stops. Below it the bass moves in short, detached notes: it dances.

Each bass note is followed by a rest, so the low line steps lightly under the running sixteenths instead of holding them up.

source: Benary (2005), p. 28

## @0:45.5 · analysis
A [[pedal-point|pedal point]] on A, the dominant, in bars 27–29: the bass holds while the harmony moves.

The music waits on the dominant before coming home to D. The held A returns briefly in bar 32.

From bar 33 the running stops: the prelude ends in the free [[stylus-phantasticus|stylus phantasticus]].

The tempo is the player's to choose. Whether only the first chord of bar 34 is broken, or every chord up to the end, is left to the performer as well.

## @1:15 · listen
The fugue's subject is the whole piece in one gesture: a rush of fast notes, then dotted rhythms.

Bach's biographer Philipp Spitta heard in it a sudden surge and a solemn grandeur.

## @1:15-@1:41 · analysis
The four voices enter from the bottom up: bass, tenor, alto, then soprano.

The second and fourth entries are the answer, on the dominant. The exposition is over by bar 6.

screen: Four voices | They enter one by one, from the bass up.

source: Lemuel Maldonado, "Analysis of Bach's fugue BWV 850 in D major", teoria.com (2011)

## @1:41 · analysis
Little strict counterpoint for a fugue: no regular countersubject, rarely all four voices at once.

A figure of four sixteenths, first heard at the end of bar 3, drives the episodes between the entries.

source: Benary (2005), p. 29

## @2:10 · trivia
The Netherlands Bach Society hears the subject as a deep courtly bow, the hat swept to the floor.

Such bows were the custom of the Polish nobility, whose king from 1697 was the Elector of Saxony.

aside: A grand gesture | Some hear the subject as a courtly bow, hat swept to the floor.

source: Netherlands Bach Society, *All of Bach*, BWV 850 (2022)
