# Overview

Bach's [[well-tempered-clavier|Prelude in C]], note for note, with a melody on top. Charles Gounod improvised the tune over the prelude as a young man in Paris, published it in 1853 as a *Méditation*, and saw it become one of the most popular pieces of the century once the words of the [[ave-maria|Ave Maria]] were set to it.

The piano plays Bach's broken chords throughout; the violin sings one long line above them. In this edition the prelude goes round twice: the second time the melody is the same, and the piano runs on to Bach's own final bars.

Gounod's copy of Bach had one bar that Bach never wrote, added by an editor around 1800. The melody needs it, so it is here, both times.

The piano begins alone with the first four bars of Bach's prelude, unchanged.

Each bar is one chord, broken into the same figure and played twice: tonic, a chord on D, the dominant seventh, tonic again.

## m3 · history
Gounod published this in Paris in 1853, for violin or cello with piano.

The first title was *Méditation sur le 1er prélude de piano de S. Bach*. Gounod is said to have improvised the melody over the prelude, and his future father-in-law, the pianist Pierre-Joseph-Guillaume Zimmerman, to have written it down.

aside: Paris, 1853 | Gounod improvises a melody over Bach's first prelude.

source: Wikipedia, "Ave Maria (Bach/Gounod)"

## m17 · history
The Well-Tempered Clavier was first printed in 1801, more than fifty years after Bach's death.

Bach's preludes and fugues had circulated in handwritten copies. Three publishers brought out editions that year, in Bonn, Zurich and Leipzig.

## m27-m28 · history
This bar is not Bach's. The melody reaches a high C on it.

Bach goes straight from the F sharp [[diminished-seventh|diminished seventh]] (here bar 26) to the chord over A flat (bar 28). An editor around 1800, usually named as Christian Friedrich Gottlieb Schwencke, added a bar of his own in between, and most 19th-century editions printed it. Gounod's copy had it, and his melody is built on it.

screen: The extra bar | An editor added it to Bach. Gounod's melody needs it.

source: Ledbetter (2002); see the notes on Bach's prelude

## m29 · history
In Rome around 1840, Fanny Mendelssohn Hensel played Bach for the young Gounod.

Gounod had won the Prix de Rome in 1839. In Rome he met Fanny Hensel, Felix Mendelssohn's sister, who introduced him to the music of Bach and Beethoven.

aside: Rome, 1840 | Fanny Mendelssohn plays Bach for the young Gounod.

source: Wikipedia, "Charles Gounod"

## m42 · trivia
The words came in 1859: the Latin Ave Maria, the prayer to the Virgin Mary.

Sung, the piece became one of the most popular songs of the 19th century. It has been arranged for almost every instrument since.

aside: 1859 | The melody gets its words: the Latin Ave Maria.

source: Wikipedia, "Ave Maria (Bach/Gounod)"

## m50 · trivia
1859 was also the year of Gounod's opera *Faust*, first staged in Paris that March.

*Faust* became one of the most performed operas of its time, and made Gounod famous across Europe.

aside: Paris, 1859 | The same year, Gounod's opera Faust opens.

source: Wikipedia, "Faust (opera)"

## m55 · trivia
Schubert's Ave Maria is a different song.

Franz Schubert's *Ellens dritter Gesang* (D. 839, 1825), a setting of Walter Scott's *The Lady of the Lake*, is also sung to the words of the Ave Maria. The two are often confused.

aside: Not Schubert's | Schubert's Ave Maria, from 1825, is a different song.

source: Wikipedia, "Ellens dritter Gesang"
