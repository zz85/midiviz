## Collection 3 — Melody and Harmony

*Eight songs without words, from Paris to Moscow to Brno, 1848–1914*

A melody you can sing back, with harmony that turns somewhere you don't expect. The programme starts in daylight, darkens through Schumann and late Liszt, and ends with Grieg remembering the very first tune of Quiet Discoveries.

### Programme notes in brief

1. **Boulanger.** A bright opening from a composer who died at 24, and the first woman to win the Prix de Rome. The harmony glows rather than resolves.
2. **Fauré.** A long-breathed melody while the inner voices slide by half steps underneath. A teenage piece that already sounds like Fauré.
3. **Ravel.** The tune spells H-A-Y-D-N in notes, then comes back backwards and upside down, all inside a polite minuet.
4. **Janáček.** A tune shaped like a folk song keeps swerving into unexpected keys, the way speech changes direction mid-sentence.
5. **Schumann.** The programme's strange centre: a hovering, chromatic bird call framing a hymn-like middle section.
6. **Liszt.** Seventy years old, Liszt writes a bare melody over chords that belong to no key, and stops without an ending. The darkest point of the programme.
7. **Rachmaninoff.** After *Nuages gris*, light returns: a still, high melody over rippling accompaniment, unusually calm for Rachmaninoff.
8. **Grieg.** His last Lyric Piece quotes the *Arietta* he wrote 34 years earlier as a fading waltz. It closes both this collection and the arc that began with Quiet Discoveries.

**Reserves:** Debussy *Canope* · Liszt *En rêve*, S. 207 · Brahms Intermezzo Op. 76 No. 3 · Chopin Prelude Op. 28 No. 13 · Scriabin Prelude Op. 11 No. 13 · Rachmaninoff Étude-tableau Op. 33 No. 8 (already in the library)

## Collection 4 — Bravura

*Eight showpieces most audiences haven't heard, 1829–1911*

Medium-to-advanced pieces that excite without the usual encores. The order climbs: light fingerwork first, then sparkle, storm and fairy-tale menace, and finally the twentieth century arrives with Bartók and Prokofiev.

### Programme notes in brief

1. **Mendelssohn.** Written at twenty: quick, light and transparent. An easy entry to the collection.
2. **Moszkowski.** Once in every great pianist's encores, now rarely played. Repeated notes flicker like sparks.
3. **Schumann.** Feathery figuration that never settles, interrupted by a slow, hymn-like middle.
4. **Chopin.** The hardest of the Preludes: six crashing chords, then one breathless minute of running notes.
5. **Liszt.** Gnomes dart in staccato, then the middle section turns fierce and harmonically wild.
6. **Rachmaninoff.** A storm in under two minutes. MIDI already in the library.
7. **Bartók.** The piano played like a drum, with hammered chords over a driving ostinato. Its 1911 audience had heard nothing like it.
8. **Prokofiev.** The finale: a seventeen-year-old's dissonant, leaping, diabolical toccata.

**Reserves:** Alkan *Toccatina* (already built, 1:29) · Grieg *Butterfly*, Op. 43 No. 1 · Scriabin Étude Op. 42 No. 5 · Debussy *Les collines d'Anacapri* · Mendelssohn *Spinning Song*, Op. 67 No. 4 · Moszkowski Étude Op. 72 No. 6
