# 06 · The bow arm: how it should move, how the great players move it, and how ours moves

Research note for #037 / #038 / #051 (13 Nov 2026), after the look of the right
arm in the videos: the elbow seems to move too much. `01_violinist_biomechanics.md`
§4 holds the static numbers (bow dimensions, the three holds, elbow height per
string, wrist and pronation along the bow); `05_bow_hand.md` the hold and the
hand's solver. This note is about **motion**: what moves to carry the bow from
frog to tip and from string to string, what stays still, what the measured and
taught practice says, and what the rig does instead. Tags as in 01: **[V]**
checked in a source fetched for this note, **[T]** teaching practice with no
measurement found, **[calc]** derived here, **[M]** measured on the rig here.

## 1. The textbook bow arm

### 1.1 The posture: the square and the levels

- **The square** (Galamian; Fischer's *Basics* calls it "the Armenian-American
  teacher Ivan Galamian's square"): with the bow at its middle, parallel to the
  bridge, the upper arm and forearm make a right angle, the bow is parallel to
  the upper arm and the strings parallel to the forearm. From that position a
  down-bow reaches the point "with the arm neither too straight nor too bent"
  [V, Fischer 2010; Strings Magazine fig. 1].
- **Three points of elevation**: the knuckles, the wrist and the elbow. Fischer's
  rule is Aristotle's: the middle course, "the elbow more or less level with
  the bow"; a wrist raised above the forearm at the frog "takes one component of
  your bow arm out of the horizontal plane" [V, Fischer 2010; Haslop 2009].
- **Fit the violin to the bow arm**, not the other way round: the tilt of the
  violin is "as much as necessary but as little as possible"; a flat violin
  frees the bow arm and all the lifted strokes, a tilted one makes the bow
  vertical on the E string and the arm cannot rest into it [V, Fischer 2010].
- **The shoulder stays down and open**; the arm's weight, not pressure, makes
  the tone (Karen Tuttle's viola school: "the elbow drops naturally, not forced
  down, just uninstructed") [V, Rozanna's/Tuttle]. Raising the right shoulder,
  which beginners do on up-bows, raises the glenohumeral joint force in every
  direction (4 experienced violinists, mocap + musculoskeletal simulation)
  [V, Saffert et al. 2021].
- **Elbow height per string**: upper arm, forearm and bow in one plane,
  Plummer's "plate of glass under the arm and bow"; the elbow at the level of
  the string played, high for the G string, low for the E [V, Strings Magazine;
  01 §4.3 table: 60–75° of abduction on G, 30–40° on E].

### 1.2 Who moves for what

| Part of the stroke | What moves | What stays |
|---|---|---|
| Lower half (frog → middle) | the upper arm swings forward from the shoulder; the elbow angle changes little | the forearm's angle to the upper arm |
| Upper half (middle → tip) | the forearm opens at the elbow; the upper arm adds a small forward motion that turns the forearm's arc into a straight line; the hand pronates and the index takes weight | the elbow's height; the shoulder (a little protraction at the very tip) |
| Bow change | the fingers and the wrist absorb the reversal; at the frog the fingers give 1–2 cm | the arm |
| Slow string crossing | the whole arm rises or falls to the new level, led by the elbow ("the elbow leads"; violin/viola: the hand leads down to a lower string, the elbow leads up to a higher one) | the hand's shape on the bow |
| Fast crossings (bariolage, arpeggios) | wrist and fingers rock the bow | the arm, parked at the middle level between the strings used |
| Fast détaché, sautillé | forearm and wrist only | the upper arm |

Sources: 01 §4.4, §4.3; [V, Schallock 2025] ("no single, isolated motion will
result in a straight line ... a primary motion, such as the pivot of the forearm
around the elbow, and a supplemental motion, such as the forward motion of the
upper arm during the latter part of the down-bow"); [V, Moyer & Woods, CASMEC];
[V, Howes]; [V, Rozanna's string crossing].

A measured detail: in an accelerando of détaché the stroke changes abruptly from
a rectangular to a sinusoidal velocity profile, and at the fastest rates the
wrist and elbow go from anti-phase to in-phase; fast strokes are forearm + wrist
[V, Rasamimanana et al. 2009, via 01 §4.4]. In fast crossing patterns the
crossing leads the bow change by about 15° of phase [V, Schoonderwaldt &
Altenmüller 2014].

### 1.3 What skill looks like, measured

- **Experts move less at the shoulder.** Learning the violin "was not
  associated with a release or freeing of joint degrees of freedom. Instead,
  learning was characterized by an experience-dependent suppression of sagittal
  shoulder motion"; the other elbow and shoulder degrees of freedom showed
  neither; only players past ~700 practice hours reached experts' shoulder range
  [V, Konczak, Vander Velden & Jaeger 2009].
- **Almost all the motion is in the right arm**; the spine, thorax, neck and
  left arm of 12 professionals were "quite static" [V, Wolf et al. 2019, via 01].
- **Straightness is approximate.** Rounded bow paths and crooked bowing at the
  tip appear even in renowned players, and skewness of ±10° is used on purpose to
  move the contact point [V, Schoonderwaldt 2009, after Hodgson]. A perfectly
  straight bow is a teaching ideal, not what the great players show.
- **Standing** gives more right-shoulder abduction on the low strings (max
  47–74° vs 41–65° sitting) and a little less elbow flexion (max 95–112° vs
  100–113°) [V, Roy et al. 2026, via 01].

### 1.4 The great players: schools and philosophy

- **Russian (Auer → Heifetz, Milstein; Soviet variant Oistrakh, Kogan).** Index
  deep on the stick, wrist high at the frog, so the elbow sits a little higher;
  but "Heifetz's elbow still only rarely rose above his wrist" [V, Fiddlerman].
  The signature is economy: Milstein's wrist keeps "almost the same natural
  angle from heel to tip", and Heifetz's nearly so [V, Violinist.com 2013]. Flat
  violin (Heifetz, Szeryng) goes with a higher elbow; a slanted one (Oistrakh)
  with a lower [V, Violinist.com 2006]. Kogan and Oistrakh lift the index at
  the frog and the little finger at the tip [V, Violinist.com 2016].
- **Franco-Belgian (Ysaÿe, Grumiaux, Perlman; most players today).** Index at
  the first/second joint, medium wrist, the elbow level with the bow; the most
  flexible, springy fingers; the default of 01 §4.2.
- **Galamian (US conservatories).** A synthesis: the square, "the arm is a
  system of springs", every joint a little flexed so none locks; motion from
  the biggest unit that can do the job, the smaller ones correcting. His "level"
  rule is the elbow at the height of the string.
- **Tuttle / Primrose (viola).** Weight and release rather than grip; the bow
  arm hangs from a low, open shoulder; the elbow uninstructed. A viola is
  bigger, its bow heavier (~70 g vs ~60) and its C string wants more tilt (to
  60°) and more force [V, Schoonderwaldt 2009, via 01 §2.5]; the right elbow
  runs a little higher and the strokes slower and nearer the bridge [T].
- **The common philosophy**: the arm is a chain of levers that cannot draw a
  straight line, so the stroke is a *coordination* of arcs; big muscles (back
  and shoulder) supply power and set the level, small ones (wrist, fingers)
  steer and absorb; nothing is held, nothing hikes up; the player chooses the
  minimum motion that keeps the bow where the sound needs it (O'Reilly: "we
  function like baseball pitchers") [V, Strings Magazine; Schallock 2025].

### 1.5 Numbers to animate by [calc from the above, 01 §4.3–4.6]

For an arm of upper arm l₁ and forearm l₂ (a 180 cm man: 33 + 26 cm; our VRoid
models: 22 + 25 cm):

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Elbow height below the shoulder | E: −l₁·cos 35°, A: −l₁·cos 45°, D: −l₁·cos 55°, G: −l₁·cos 68° (for l₁ = 22: −18, −16, −13, −8 cm) |
| Elbow travel per string, slow crossing | ≈ 2.5–5 cm at this arm; it moves over 150–300 ms with the arm, leading the bow change by ~15° of phase |
| Elbow travel per crossing, fast patterns (> 4 crossings/s) | ≈ 0 (parked at the middle level of the strings in use); the bow rocks from the wrist and fingers |
| Elbow path / hand path, lower half | ≈ l₁ / (l₁ + l₂·cos(flex − 90°)) ≈ 0.6–0.7 (the arm swings as a unit) |
| Elbow path / hand path, upper half | ≈ 0.15–0.25 (the forearm opens; the elbow drifts a few cm forward) |
| Elbow flexion | frog 95–110°, middle ≈ 90°, tip 20–40° (interior 140–160°) |
| Elbow swivel (position on the shoulder–wrist circle) | in the arm-level plane through the bow axis, a few degrees below it at the frog; it changes with the string level, slowly, and not with the bow position |
| Shoulder | fixed height; protracts 2–4 cm at the tip only; never rises on up-bows |
| Wrist | +15–30° flexed at the frog → neutral → −10° at the tip (Milstein: nearly constant) |

## 2. How our violinist does it [M]

Method: `037_violinist.html?shot` driven headless (Playwright, SwiftShader) at
60 fps from t = 4 s for 30 s, recording the right shoulder, elbow and wrist in
world space with the bow's string `s` and position `pos` from `poseAt`
(`violinist/test/bowarm.mjs`, which prints and does not judge). The default
model: arm 22.0 + 24.9 cm, 181 cm tall, bow use scaled by `bowReach` = 0.81
(05 §3).

### 2.1 What is right

- **Elbow height per string** matches the research for this arm: median
  −12.3 cm on D, −15.9 on A, −18.4 on E (Preludio); −13.3 / −15.9 / −18.0
  (Adagio). The target for l₁ = 22 cm is −13 / −16 / −18. The level rule works.
- **Elbow flexion** stays in the measured range (5th–95th percentile 72–125°
  in the Adagio, 110–128° in the Preludio), never straight (05 §2 fixed that).
- **The shoulder** does not rise with the stroke; the clavicle protracts only
  near the tip.
- **Wrist** within the 05 §4 budget (not re-measured here).

### 2.2 What is wrong

**(a) The elbow follows every string crossing at any speed.** In the Preludio
the bow crosses strings on 194 of 1800 frames and on those frames the elbow
moves 1.35 cm/frame (81 cm/s) against the wrist's 2.63 cm/frame. The level
term in `bowHandPose` is `-55 + 22 * level` with `level = 1 − s/3` from the
*float* string, unsmoothed, so a 16th-note bariolage at 8 notes/s pumps the
elbow through its full 3 cm per string on each note: 5.7 elbow-height
reversals per second in the Preludio. A player parks the arm and rocks the
bow from the wrist above about 4 crossings/s (§1.2). This is the motion that
reads as "too much elbow".

**(b) The elbow travels half the hand's path on every stroke, wherever the bow
is.** Elbow path / wrist path: 0.53 (Preludio), 0.62 (Romance), 0.74 (Adagio),
and the same 0.51–0.66 ratio on crossing, stroke and still frames alike. On
strokes with the bow past 35 cm, where the forearm should do the work, the
ratio is still 0.36 (Romance) and 0.43 (Adagio) against the textbook ≈ 0.2.
The upper half does not exist in the rig because:
  - the arm is short for the bow (47 vs ~59 cm) and the bow use is scaled to
    0.81, so the hand never gets far enough from the shoulder to open the
    elbow: median flexion 118° in the Preludio, which is the *frog* region of a
    real arm, where the upper arm does the moving; the "square" (90°) is never
    reached at the middle of the bow;
  - the swivel pole is a fixed offset from the shoulder, `S + (−30, −55 + 22·level + 12·tip, −12)`,
    so the elbow sits 28–35° behind the straight-down position about the
    shoulder–wrist axis and swings with the hand as a rigid unit.

**(c) Pops at the frog and the tip.** In the Adagio, 21 frames move the elbow
more than 3 cm in one frame, 12.6 cm at most (758 cm/s; swivel rate 2300°/s),
at `pos` 4–7 cm (frog) and 61–62 cm (tip). The hand-pose cost alternates on
consecutive frames (5.6 → 318 → 9.8 → 287 → 8 → 328 at t = 23.8 s): the
coordinate descent flips between two basins, `BH.x` is lerped between them (a
blend of two valid poses is itself a bad pose), and `elbowFor`'s torso-clearance
loop moves the pole in 25 cm steps when the forearm comes within `CLEAR` of the
torso, so the elbow toggles between "in" and "kicked out". Four such frames in
the Romance, none in the Preludio (which never reaches the frog or the tip).

**(d) The swivel is fast on ordinary strokes.** Rate of the elbow about the
shoulder–wrist axis: 95th percentile 102°/s in the Preludio (max 177°/s),
40°/s in the Adagio. Real elbows change their swivel only with the string
level, over hundreds of milliseconds.

**(e) Not tested.** `violinist/test/posture.mjs` checks the bow elbow's flexion
(60–165°) and nothing about its motion, so none of (a)–(d) fails a test.

### 2.3 What would fix it

In order of how much it would change the look:

1. **Low-pass the string level for the arm, and hand the fast part to the
   wrist.** Split `s` into `sArm` (first-order filter, τ ≈ 150–250 ms, or the
   mean of the strings used in the last 300 ms when crossings come faster than
   4/s) and the remainder `s − sArm`, which the bow already shows by its
   inclination; tilt the hand about the grip point by the remainder's share of
   the 21.7° per string so the wrist rocks the bow. The elbow then moves
   2.5–5 cm per slow crossing and stays put in a bariolage.
2. **Pole in the arm-level plane** (01 §6.3 step 9), not a fixed offset from
   the shoulder: the plane through the shoulder that contains the bow axis,
   the elbow below the shoulder–wrist line by 8–12 cm at the frog, 3 cm at the
   middle, 0–2 cm at the tip. Low-pass the pole (50–100 ms). This stops the
   swivel following the hand.
3. **Give the arm a bow it fits.** Either scale the bow to the arm (a 0.8 bow
   for a 0.8 arm, as the violin is already scaled by `V.k` for the hands) so
   the elbow reaches 90° at the middle and 140–150° at the tip, or lengthen the
   arm bones' IK lengths toward 0.9 of the bow (the sleeve hides a few cm).
   With the square restored, the upper half becomes forearm-led by geometry.
4. **No blending between basins.** When the descent restarts from the nominal
   pose and the better result lies in another basin (cost ratio > 3 and
   |Δx| large), switch over 2–3 frames along the *lower-cost* basin's path, not
   by lerping `x`; replace the 25 cm torso kick with a continuous penalty on
   the forearm–torso gap inside `evaluate` so the elbow slides out instead of
   jumping.
5. **Test it.** Add to `posture.mjs`, over 20 s at 60 fps while the bow is on
   the string: elbow speed ≤ 60 cm/s on 99 % of frames and no frame > 3 cm;
   the elbow's distance from its own 0.2 s mean ≤ 2 cm on 95 % of frames;
   elbow path / wrist path ≤ 0.45 over fast crossings between adjacent strings
   in the upper half of the bow, and ≤ 0.3 for strokes whose `pos` stays above
   35 cm. (The swivel rate of §2.2 d is not a budget: the shoulder–wrist axis
   it is measured about swings with the hand, so a still elbow reads as a fast
   swivel on a fast stroke.)

### 2.4 After fix 1 (14 Nov 2026) [M]

What changed in `bowHandPose`: the arm's level comes from `st.sArm`, a
first-order follower of the bowed string (τ 0.18 s on the string, 0.06 s off
it); the elbow is placed where that level would put it with the bow un-rocked
about its contact point (the wrist rotated back about the strings' axis by the
tilt difference), then kept at the point of the real wrist's two-bone circle
nearest that; and the wrist's wanted bend takes 0.3 of the rock's lever (the
hand's distance from the contact over the forearm, fitted at −0.25 to the
solver's own residual; the fingers and the hand's slide on the stick take the
rest), ±15° at most. The sign and share were fitted, not assumed: the opposite
un-rock made the crossing ratio 0.77.

| Preludio, 30 s | before | after | textbook |
|---|---|---|---|
| elbow path / wrist path | 0.53 | 0.38 | 0.2–0.7 by bow region |
| elbow on crossing frames, cm/frame (ratio to wrist) | 1.35 (0.51) | 0.66 (0.27) | ≈ 0 in a bariolage |
| elbow speed, 95th pct | 91 cm/s | 43 cm/s | |
| elbow from its 0.2 s mean, 95th pct / max | 1.9 / 2.6 cm | 1.1 / 1.8 cm | |
| elbow height D / A / E (median) | −12.3 / −15.9 / −18.4 | −13.5 / −16.2 / −18.3 | −13 / −16 / −18 |
| posture.mjs fast-crossing ratio (upper half) | 0.48 FAIL | 0.21 | ≤ 0.45 |

Not changed by fix 1, and now measured as pending in `posture.mjs`: the
one-frame jumps (Adagio 5, Romance 4, all at the frog or the tip with the
solver's cost toggling 6 ↔ 300) and the elbow speed over 60 cm/s (Paganini 11 %
of frames); the lower-half crossing ratio (Paganini 0.68, Ciaccona 0.78),
which is geometric with the arm folded past 100°: the hand's rock then has a
component along the forearm that the elbow must follow, and the fix is the
bow fitted to the arm (step 3). Clips: `violinist/tools/render_clip.mjs`, the
Preludio from 4 s with the camera at (−170, 165, 110) → (−5, 133, 20), fov 34.
